#3. Methodology

##3.2. AROMA Pipeline
The AROMA pipeline (Figure 3.2) progresses from dataset profiling and complexity analysis to defect synthesis and quality control. Unlike CASDA, which relies on a handcrafted, do-main-specific compatibility matrix and morphological rules, AROMA re-derives its de-fect–background compatibility model directly from dataset statistics. By profiling defect morphology and background context, AROMA builds this compatibility model from patch-level co-occurrence and summarizes each dataset's difficulty with the Morphology Complexity Index (MCI) and Context Complexity Index (CCI), without manual tuning.
###Figure 3.2. AROMA Pipeline

###3.2.1 Dataset Complexity Analysis
The pipeline first quantifies dataset complexity with two complementary indices. The Morphology Complexity Index (MCI) summarizes the structural variability of defect regions as the mean of four normalized statistics capturing intensity variation, distribution multimodality, defect-type diversity, and cluster separability. The Context Complexity Index (CCI) measures background heterogeneity as the mean of four normalized components:
CCI = Mean(TextureEntropy, ContextClusterCount, FreqComplexity, OrientVariance),

where the components quantify texture disorder, the number of background context clusters, frequency-domain complexity, and gradient-orientation variability, estimated from 20,000 sampled image patches per dataset. Both indices characterize the evaluation roster; the CCI additionally conditions the downstream analysis of when context-aware placement is beneficial (Sections 4.2–4.3).

As shown in Table 2, decomposing the CCI into its four components reveals the background characteristics of each dataset. Orientation variance and frequency complex-ity provide the strongest discrimination, with AITeX exhibiting the highest values due to its directional, periodic texture, whereas Kolektor and MVTec Leather show consistently low values characteristic of smooth, uniform surfaces. In contrast, texture entropy varies only modestly (2.58–3.22), indicating that structural rather than intensity-based features dominate background differentiation. These measurements provide the quantitative basis for the background categories used during ROI extraction (Section 3.2.3).

Kolektor	Metal	0.224	2.578	5	0.0002	0.0035

###3.2.2 Morphology and Context Modeling
AROMA models defect morphology and background context with per-dataset, data-driven partitions that require no hand-set constants. Defect instances are grouped by a Gaussian mixture whose number of clusters is selected per dataset by the Bayesian In-formation Criterion (BIC), yielding morphology clusters that adapt to each dataset's defect population. Background context is discretized into cells by per-feature tertile (P33/P66) binning of the profiled context features. These morphology clusters and context cells form the row and column indices of the symmetric compatibility model used for placement (Section 3.2.4); because both partitions are estimated from each dataset's own statistics, no dataset-specific constants are introduced.

As shown in Figure 3.2.2-1, defect shape varies mostly along an elongation continuum—from compact, low-aspect blobs to thin, near-linear scratches—so the resulting morphology clusters are essentially segments of this spectrum; each cluster k carries a prior P(k) = n_k / N, its share of the defect population, that serves as the morph_prior when ranking placements (Section 3.2.4).

**Figure 3.2.2-1.** Data-driven morphology clusters for Severstal and AITeX: each defect in the linearity–log(aspect ratio) plane, colored by its shape cluster k (cluster centroids ★; the legend gives each cluster's label and prior P(k) = morph_prior). The clusters are segments of an elongation continuum; k is a shape cluster, not the defect class.

As shown in Figure 3.2.2-2, the five profiled context features and their P33/P66 tertile boundaries differ markedly across datasets, so the same discretization yields dataset-specific context cells; the structure-sensitive features (frequency energy, orientation en-tropy) vary most across the roster, consistent with the CCI decomposition (see §3.2.1).

**Figure 3.2.2-2.** Per-dataset distributions of the five profiled background context features (one panel per dataset), with the P33/P66 tertile boundaries (dashed) that discretize each feature into the three-level context cells indexing the compatibility model.

###3.2.3 Background Categories and Defect Subtypes
Regions of interest (ROIs) were extracted from normal images to identify plausible defect placement locations: Otsu thresholding produces a foreground mask, connected-component analysis yields candidate ROIs, and components below a minimum connected-component area are discarded as noise. The texture surrounding each retained ROI is classified into one of five categories—smooth, directional, periodic, organic, or complex—by the per-dataset percentile cascade of Table 3, and a continuity score and a stability score quantify each ROI's spatial coherence and its consistency across the normal-image population.

###Table 3. Background texture categories from ROI extraction.

Category	Computational Criteria	Example Surface
Smooth	LocalVariance ≤ P25	bare metal, uniform coating
Directional	GradOrientEntropy ≤ P25
AND AutocorrPeak ≤ P25	textile weave
Periodic	AutocorrPeak ≥ P75	tiles, checker patterns
Organic	LBPEntropy ≥ P50
AND LocalVariance ≥ P75	leather, wood, fabric
Complex	else	Boundaries, composite surfaces

The four descriptors of Table 3 are computed on the same 64-pixel patch grid as the context cells (Section 3.2.2), from the grayscale patch p alone. LocalVariance is the intensity variance of p. Two of the remaining descriptors are the Shannon entropy [36] of a normalized histogram q,

Entropy(q) = − Σ_k q_k · log₂ q_k,

applied to two different histograms. GradOrientEntropy takes the histogram of Sobel gradient orientations over eight 22.5° bins, each orientation reduced modulo 180° so that the two opposite gradient signs along one stripe share a bin, and each weighted by its gradient magnitude [37]; it is smallest when a single edge orientation dominates, as on a woven or brushed surface. LBPEntropy takes the ten-bin histogram of the rotation-invariant uniform local binary pattern with eight neighbours at radius one [38]; it is largest where many micro-patterns coexist without a dominant one, as on grained or fibrous surfaces. AutocorrPeak is the strongest off-origin peak of the patch autocorrelation R, normalized by its value at the origin [39],

AutocorrPeak(p) = max_{(u,v) ≠ (0,0)} R(u,v) / R(0,0),   where R = ℱ⁻¹(|ℱ(p)|²),

with ℱ the two-dimensional Fourier transform, so that AutocorrPeak is largest when the patch reproduces itself at some non-zero lag, as on a tiled or checkered surface. Although obtained through the Fourier transform, it measures spatial self-similarity and is therefore distinct from the frequency-energy component of the CCI (Section 3.2.1).

The five categories are assigned by a lazy cascade in the order listed in Table 3, the first satisfied criterion winning, so each criterion is implicitly conjoined with the negation of all preceding ones; patches satisfying none of the four are labelled Complex.

Seed defects were extracted from defective images to provide morphological templates for synthesis. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) [31] generates precise defect masks, with Otsu thresholding as a fallback when SAM segmentation is unavailable or unreliable. Each defect is classified into one of four generic morphological subtypes—linear_scratch, compact_blob, irregular, or general—using the threshold criteria of Table 4 on aspect ratio and solidity, whose numeric values are derived from each dataset's own defect population (Table 4b); these subtype labels parameterize the subtype-specific elastic-warping variants generated before composition, without relying on domain-specific defect categories.

**Table 4.** Defect subtype classification criteria

| Subtype | Classification Criteria (evaluated in order, first match wins) | Description |
|---------|---------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| linear_scratch | AspectRatio > P66(AspectRatio) | long, thin, near-linear defect |
| compact_blob | AspectRatio < P33(AspectRatio) AND Solidity > P66(Solidity) | compact, nearly isotropic defect with a convex boundary |
| irregular | Solidity < P33(Solidity) | defect with a ragged or strongly concave boundary |
| general | default label (no criterion above satisfied) | fallback for ambiguous or mixed-morphology defects |

The thresholds are the P33 and P66 tertiles of each dataset's own defect population — the same discretization already applied to the background context features (Section 3.2.2), so that every partition in the pipeline is anchored to the dataset it describes. The rule is therefore dataset-invariant in form while its numeric values are estimated per dataset; the derived values are reported in Table 4b. A single fixed constant cannot define the same morphological category across the roster: an aspect ratio of 5.0 sits at the 40th percentile of Kolektor's defect population but the 78th of MVTec Leather's, and a solidity of 0.9 at the 10th percentile of Kolektor's but the 67th of AITeX's.

The criteria involve only two features, both standard region shape descriptors [40]. The solidity of the defect region is the ratio of its actual area to the area of its convex hull, and therefore falls as a boundary becomes more ragged or strongly concave. For the aspect ratio, an ellipse is fitted to the defect region and the ratio of its major to minor axis is taken; the fit is obtained from the region's second central moments [41], which makes the aspect ratio a function of the same eigenvalues that define linearity. Writing lambda_max and lambda_min for the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix of the region's pixel coordinates, AspectRatio = sqrt(lambda_max/lambda_min) while Linearity = 1 - lambda_min/lambda_max, so that

Linearity = 1 - AspectRatio^-2.

Linearity is therefore not used as a criterion: every linearity threshold reduces to an aspect-ratio threshold, and the two features order the defect population identically (Spearman rho = 1.000 on all five datasets). Eccentricity, which equals the square root of Linearity, is redundant in the same sense; all three are profiled and reported, but only aspect ratio enters the classification.

The cascade order is load-bearing where the first and third criteria overlap — an elongated defect with a concave boundary is labelled linear_scratch, not irregular. One safeguard is applied to the tertile estimation: when a dataset's middle tertile for a feature spans less than 15% of that feature's standard deviation, the dataset is morphologically homogeneous in that feature and splitting it on tertiles would encode measurement noise rather than shape, so that feature alone reverts to a fixed threshold while the other keeps its data-derived tertiles. Across the roster this applies to solidity on Kolektor only, whose middle tertile spans 0.0029 — 3.4% of the standard deviation, against 58–118% for the other four datasets.

The derived thresholds vary by a factor of 4.5 in aspect ratio (3.62 for MTD to 16.43 for AITeX) and span 0.648 to 0.981 in solidity, so the same rule expresses a markedly different shape boundary in each domain. This is the morphological variation that a fixed constant would absorb silently.

As shown in Figure 3.2.3-1, the per-dataset distributions of the profiled morphology features locate the Table 4 thresholds within each dataset's own shape distribution: the solidity thresholds fall in sparsely populated regions, whereas the aspect-ratio thresholds necessarily cut a continuously populated range, and each dataset occupies a distinct morphological regime.

###3.2.4 ROI Selection and Compatibility-Aware Placement

ROI selection ranks all candidate defect–context pairs by a compatibility score and retains the top-K; a base quota per morphology cluster guarantees coverage, and for multi-class datasets a uniform per-class floor prevents any class from being starved. Candidates are ordered by

ROI_score = 0.6 · ctx_prior + 0.4 · morph_prior,

where ctx_prior is the profiled defect–background compatibility defined below, and morph_prior is the morphology-cluster prior P(k) of Section 3.2.2. Both priors are derived directly from the profiling statistics, so the ranking introduces no hand-set constants; the compatibility term dominates the score, while the cluster prior mainly orders candidates whose compatibility is saturated (ctx_prior = 1). To prevent morphological collapse, each source defect crop is selected at most once. Before synthesis, a clean background is assigned to each ROI by ranking candidate backgrounds using histogram-matching cues derived from the profiling stage, including source overlap, class-conditional overlap, and size compatibility.

During composition, placement is governed by a symmetric compatibility gate that scores a defect morphology cluster k against a 64-pixel context cell c by the geometric mean of two profiled occurrence probabilities:

ctx_prior(k, c) ∝ √(P_def(k, c) · P_clean(c)),

where k indexes a morphology cluster and c a context cell. Both factors are cell-frequency distributions over the same discrete cell space, each obtained by counting 64-pixel background patches and normalising the counts to sum to one. P_clean(c) is estimated over the patches of all normal images, whereas P_def(k, c) is estimated over the patches of those defect images whose defect belongs to cluster k, each patch inheriting its image's cluster label; patches overlapping a defect region are excluded during profiling, so P_def describes the background context surrounding cluster k's defects rather than the defects themselves. The square root of the product is the geometric mean of the two factors: it is symmetric in them and collapses to zero if either vanishes, which is what the additive smoothing below prevents. The relation is written as a proportionality because the score that is stored is this geometric mean divided by its per-cluster maximum, so that each cluster's most compatible cell equals 1. Additive smoothing (ε = 10⁻³) is applied to both factors, and each cluster's scores are rescaled so that its most compatible cell equals 1 (As shown in Figure 7, the row-normalized values are stored as matrix_symmetric). The geometric mean is high only where both probabilities are high, weighting the defect and background sides symmetrically. Each candidate defect crop is evaluated against the 64-pixel tiled neighborhood of the target ROI, and the resulting compatibility score determines the placement location. This data-driven compatibility gate automatically replaces CASDA's handcrafted compatibility matrix with a dataset-specific counterpart derived directly from the profiling statistics. In contrast, the Random baseline performs no compatibility-based ranking, differing from AROMA only in the ROI placement mechanism.

As shown in Figure 3.2.4-1, each morphology cluster's compatibility concentrates on a few context cells: on heterogeneous surfaces (AITeX, MTD) different clusters prefer different cells, whereas on the near-uniform Severstal surface all clusters share one dominant cell.

###3.2.5 Blending Synthesis
Each selected defect crop is placed at the designated bounding box on the target normal image. The same blend operator is applied identically to the AROMA and random arms, ensuring blending is not a confounding factor. For each synthesized image, the corresponding defect mask and bounding box are co-saved, providing pixel-level localization for downstream detection.

###3.2.6 Quality Gate
A quality gate filters unusable background patches before clean-background assignment and foreground-void rejection. Each candidate patch is scored by four weighted components — a blur term (Laplacian variance thresholded against a fixed cutoff), a contrast term (pixel-intensity standard deviation), a brightness term (mean intensity centered in the mid-range), and a noise term (local variance from a 5×5 mean filter) — each normalized to [0, 1]. The quality score is

quality = 0.30 · blur + 0.30 · contrast + 0.20 · brightness + 0.20 · noise,

ported from CASDA's background-extraction criterion. A patch is accepted as a usable clean background iff quality ≥ 0.7; the same score also rejects flat/void foreground regions (dark or bright) that would otherwise be mistaken for a real defect object. Retention is a fixed absolute threshold, not a per-dataset adaptive fraction.
